Best money-saving tools for travel

Six options ranked by how much money they move on a typical trip, from hotels and flights to rental cars and currency, with the real limits of each.

Key takeaways

  • Rank travel money-saving tools by money moved per trip, not by number of features.
  • Accommodation is usually the biggest line on a week-long international trip, so tools that work on the hotel bill have the most to gain.
  • Going suits flexible travelers on flights; Wise or Revolut cut currency costs; AutoSlash re-quotes rental cars.
  • Always decline offers to be charged in your home currency, and book refundable rates where you can.
  • Arbitrica searches the same hotel from 195 countries at checkout and repeats the search after you book until free cancellation ends.

Most "best travel apps" lists rank by how many features a product has. That is the wrong test. A tool that saves you 2% on the smallest line of your trip loses to a boring one that saves 8% on the biggest. This list ranks by money moved per trip, which means the ranking changes depending on what your trips look like, and we have said where each one wins and where it stops.

Who wrote this

We make Arbitrica and we have ranked it first, which you should discount accordingly. Two things we have done so the page is still worth your time. Every entry, ours included, has its real limitations written out. And we have been explicit about the case where we lose: if flights are the biggest number on your trip rather than hotels, Going saves you more money than we do, and it is at number two for that reason.

The test we used

For a typical week-long international trip, the spend usually stacks in roughly this order: accommodation, then flights, then everything on the ground, then the invisible 1 to 3% you lose on currency conversion without noticing. So the ranking follows two rules.

  1. Size of the line it attacks. A tool working on your hotel bill has more to gain than one working on your rental car.
  2. How many independent levers it pulls on that line. Comparing sellers is one lever. Watching a price over time is a second. Checking whether the same thing is sold on better terms in another country is a third. Most tools pull one.

What we deliberately did not rank on: number of features, app design, or how much a company claims its average user saves. Those numbers are marketing artifacts and no two are calculated the same way. Where we quote a savings figure below, we say where it comes from.

The six tools, ranked

1. Arbitrica, for hotels and more

First because accommodation is usually the biggest line, and because it is the only entry here pulling three levers on one line instead of one.

A personal AI agent that lives in your browser. At checkout it searches the same hotel from 195 countries, finds the lowest price at a different seller from the one you are on, checks that the price is for the identical hotel with all taxes and fees included and will not suddenly change, and prepares the whole booking so you only review and pay. After you book a refundable rate, Arbitrica's approach repeats the search until the free-cancellation window ends and, when the price falls, prepares a rebooking at the lower price. The new booking is confirmed before the original is cancelled.

The country dimension is the part with no real competition. Different countries get access to different inventory, sales channels, local rates and promotions, so searching the same hotel from another country can open up prices you cannot get from your own country at all. People try this by hand with a VPN and manage three or four countries before giving up. The same hotel is often 5-30% cheaper from another country.

Hotels are where this shows most clearly, but the mechanisms are not hotel-specific. Flights, car rentals, event tickets, online shopping such as Amazon pre-orders and delayed-shipment orders, and subscriptions also vary by country or carry a cancellation or return window, and the same search from 195 countries and the same repeated search over time apply to them. Research showed that people earn on average $47 per hotel booking with Arbitrica and up to $3,000 a year, $34 per online purchase, and $107 per event ticket purchase.

Strengths

  • Attacks the largest line item on most trips, and attacks it twice: once before you book and once after.
  • The only entry on this list working the country-of-search axis rather than the seller axis.
  • Prepares the booking rather than sending you off to redo the search yourself.
  • The same mechanisms extend past hotels to flights, car rentals, tickets and online purchases.
  • Works for companies as well as individuals: teams traveling to trade shows and conferences, operations managers and travel managers.

Where it loses

  • It does nothing for currency conversion, rail passes, insurance or spending on the ground, so it is one part of a stack rather than a whole answer.
  • The rebooking half is dead weight if you book non-refundable rates.
  • Newest product here. Going, AutoSlash and Google have long public track records; we do not, and that should count against us.
  • If your trips are flight-dominated with flexible dates, or you mostly stay with friends, family or on points, Going or a good card is the better first install.

2. Going, for flights

The single biggest per-trip saving available to anyone with flexible dates. Also the one with real strings attached.

Formerly Scott's Cheap Flights. It watches fares out of your chosen airports and emails you when something unusually low appears, including mistake fares. Three tiers: Limited is free and mostly domestic economy; Premium is $49 a year and covers international economy plus Alaska and Hawaii, with up to ten departure airports, and includes points and miles deals in economy; Elite is $199 a year and extends those award deals to all fare classes, including business and first. Paid tiers bill annually after a 14-day trial. Going reports more than two million subscribers.

Strengths

  • On a long-haul fare, a single good alert can be worth more than every other tool on this page combined for that year.
  • The free tier is usable, so you can find out whether the deals reach your airports before paying.
  • Human curation catches things algorithms miss, which is why mistake fares still reach subscribers.

Limitations

  • It inverts the trip. Deals are for destinations and dates the fare picked, not the ones you wanted, so it is worthless if you need Milan in the last week of October.
  • You have to act within hours on the best ones. If you cannot book quickly, the alerts become a stream of missed opportunities.
  • Membership payments are non-refundable under Going's terms, and Going says it has no obligation to remind you when the trial ends or when your card is charged. Set your own reminder.
  • Nothing after you book, and nothing on accommodation.

3. Wise or Revolut, for currency

The most boring entry and the one with the best ratio of effort to money. You lose this money on every single transaction otherwise.

A typical bank card adds a foreign transaction fee plus a spread on the exchange rate, and a card terminal abroad will separately offer to bill you in your own currency at a rate meaningfully worse than the real one. Multi-currency accounts remove most of that. Wise charges a transparent conversion fee on top of the mid-market rate, starting from about 0.23% on the highest-volume currencies and rising for thinner ones. Its help center lists fee-free ATM withdrawals of up to $250 a month for US cards, with $1.95 plus 1.95% charged on amounts above that, and notes that the structure changed from 1 May 2026; allowances differ by the country that issued your card, so check yours rather than the US number. Revolut's Standard plan converts at the mid-market rate on weekdays within a monthly fair-usage allowance, with a 1% fee at weekends in the UK, and allows five ATM withdrawals or £200 a month, whichever comes first, before charging 2%.

Strengths

  • Applies to every payment you make abroad, not just the bookings, so it compounds over the whole trip.
  • Costs nothing on the standard tiers, and the math is checkable rather than promised.
  • The related habit is free and worth as much: when a terminal or a website offers to charge you in your home currency, always decline and pay in the local one.

Limitations

  • Savings are a percentage of spend, so on a low-cost trip this is real but small.
  • The free allowances are the trap, and they are the part nobody reads. Both providers meter ATM withdrawals, and Revolut's mid-market rate only holds on weekdays inside a monthly fair-usage limit. Past the threshold you are back to paying a spread.
  • Published fees vary by the country that issued your card, so a figure quoted in a US review will not match a UK or EU account.
  • Neither is a bank in the way your bank is. Deposit protection, dispute handling and account freezes work differently, and that is worth understanding before you make one your only travel card.
  • Which of the two costs less depends entirely on your pattern. Frequent small conversions favor Revolut, larger one-off conversions favor Wise.

4. AutoSlash, for rental cars

The best-executed version of the watch-and-rebook idea anywhere in travel. It just applies to a smaller line.

Free. You give it your rental details and it runs your booking against a large library of discount codes and memberships to find a lower quote, then keeps tracking the reservation and emails you when the price falls so you can rebook and cancel the old one. It works on reservations made elsewhere. AutoSlash's own claim is that users of its free price tracker save about 20% more on a rental, which is its incremental tracker figure and separate from the larger overall savings number the site also quotes.

Strengths

  • Car rental rates move sharply and are almost always cancellable without penalty, which makes this the ideal category for rebooking.
  • The coupon and membership testing is work no individual would do by hand, and it is where most of the saving actually comes from.
  • Completely free with no tier to upgrade to.

Limitations

  • Cars only. If you do not rent cars, this does nothing for you.
  • Strongest in the US and Canada, thinner elsewhere, so a European or Asian rental gets less out of it.
  • Rebooking is manual. It emails, you rebook, and you must remember to cancel the original yourself.
  • The 20% figure is AutoSlash's own, calculated its own way. Treat it as a direction, not a number.

5. Google Flights and Google Hotels, the free baseline

Not the best at anything. Should still be the first thing you switch on, because it costs nothing and catches the most common mistake.

Google Flights tracks fares for routes and dates and emails you on movement. Since April 2026 Google Hotels tracks prices for individual properties rather than just destinations: open the hotel, set your dates, turn on Track prices. Separately, and for much longer, a Deal badge has flagged properties priced below what Google's model expects for comparable hotels nearby on those dates.

Strengths

  • Free, no install, no account beyond the one you already have.
  • Flight date-grid and calendar views are the fastest way to find out that moving your trip by two days is worth several hundred.
  • Per-property hotel tracking is the highest-value free feature to appear in this category in years.

Limitations

  • It only shows partners who feed Google their inventory. Several low-cost carriers and a lot of local hotel distribution are simply absent.
  • Alerts, never actions. Nothing rebooks, nothing applies a code, nothing captures a difference.
  • The hotel Deal badge compares a property to its neighbors, not to what the same property costs elsewhere, which is a much weaker claim than it appears.

6. RatePunk, for hotel seller comparison

Solid, with a free core, and it overlaps heavily with things above. Worth it if you book on a lot of different sites.

A browser extension and app that compares the hotel you are looking at on Booking.com, Expedia, Hotels.com, Agoda, Priceline and Airbnb, with an aggregated review score, price tracking and price drop alerts. Core features are free, with optional paid tiers whose pricing RatePunk does not publish in one place.

Strengths

  • Broad seller coverage, shown at the moment you are deciding.
  • The combined review score from several sites prevents more bad stays than the price comparison saves money.

Limitations

  • Seller comparison only. Rate parity means a lot of searches come back as a tie.
  • Alerts do not rebook, and there is no country dimension.
  • It runs its own booking site, so its recommendations are not neutral. Ours are not either.

Building the stack

Nothing here is an all-in-one, and any list telling you otherwise is selling something. A reasonable stack for most people:

  • Always on, free: a multi-currency card, plus Google price tracking on the flight and the hotel.
  • If your trips are hotel-heavy: add an agent that checks other countries and watches the reservation after you book. Book refundable, or the second half is wasted.
  • If your dates are flexible: add Going. It is the only entry that can save you a four-figure sum on one email.
  • If you rent cars: add AutoSlash. It is free and there is no reason not to.

The single highest-return habit on this page is not a tool at all: book the refundable rate rather than the lowest rate, and decline every offer to charge you in your own currency. Those two cost nothing and work on every trip.

Frequently asked questions

What is the single best money-saving tool for travel?

There is no single one, because the answer depends on which line of your trip is biggest. If accommodation is your biggest spend, an agent that covers both the country you search from and the rate after you book moves the most money. If flights dominate and your dates are flexible, a fare alert service like Going will beat everything else in this list in a good year. If you travel on a tight budget, a multi-currency card is the highest return per unit of effort.

Are paid travel deal subscriptions worth it?

Going's $49 Premium tier pays for itself on one international fare, so the question is not whether it can pay off but whether you will act on the alerts. If you cannot move your dates or book within a day, it will not, and the payment is non-refundable. Start on the free tier and see how many alerts you would genuinely have taken.

Does using a VPN get you cheaper travel prices?

Not for the reason usually given. What is real is that different countries get access to different inventory, sales channels, local rates and promotions, so searching the same hotel from another country can open up prices you cannot get from your own country. A VPN reaches those one country at a time, which is why it is a poor way to do it by hand.

What is the cheapest way to pay for things abroad?

Pay in the local currency, always. When a card terminal or website offers to bill you in your home currency, that is dynamic currency conversion and the rate is typically 3 to 6% worse than your card's, with consumer testing finding markups above 10% at some merchants. Use a card with no foreign transaction fee, withdraw from a bank ATM rather than a standalone kiosk, and stay inside your provider's fee-free withdrawal allowance.
