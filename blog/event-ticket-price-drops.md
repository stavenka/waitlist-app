Event ticket prices drop, and they also climb, sometimes within the same week. If you have watched a concert or a match go from expensive to more expensive and then suddenly reasonable, you have seen how ticket pricing works now. There is no single best day to buy, but there are patterns, and knowing them helps you decide when to wait and when to buy.

## Primary ticket sales vs. resale

Tickets reach you through two kinds of market.

- **Primary sales** come from the official seller for the venue, promoter or team. Many primary sellers now use dynamic pricing, so the price for the same section can rise when demand is high. Dynamic pricing drew wide public criticism during the 2024 Oasis reunion sale in the UK, when many fans saw prices rise while they waited in the queue.

- **Resale** is fans and brokers selling tickets they already hold, through marketplaces. Each seller sets a price, and the marketplace adds its own fees. Prices here move with supply and demand, sometimes hour by hour.

Some countries, events and artists limit resale above face value or tie tickets to the buyer's name. Always check the rules for the specific event before you buy on resale.

## How resale ticket prices move over time

Every event is different, but resale prices often follow a recognizable shape:

- **Right after the on-sale**When an event sells out at primary, resale listings appear quickly at high prices. Sellers test what people will pay.

- **The long middle**Prices settle as more listings come in and early demand cools. For many events this is a slow drift, not a crash.

- **The final days**This is where prices can move the most in either direction. If there are many unsold seats, sellers would rather get something than nothing, and prices can fall sharply close to the event. If the event is in high demand, the last tickets can go higher.

- **Extra releases**Promoters sometimes release more tickets or production holds closer to the date, which can pull resale prices down.

So the cheapest time to buy concert tickets depends mostly on one thing: how much supply is left relative to demand. A stadium show with thousands of listings behaves very differently from a small venue that sold out in minutes.

| Situation | What often happens to resale prices | What it means for you |

| Large venue, many listings | Gradual drift down, possible drop in the last days | Waiting can pay, if you accept some risk |

| Small venue, sold out fast | Stays high or rises | Waiting rarely helps |

| Weekday show, low buzz | Soft prices, more scope to fall | Watch listings and buy when the total suits you |

| Extra tickets announced | Resale prices can dip | Check primary first, then resale |

| You need specific seats together | Fewer matching listings, less price movement | Buy earlier rather than later |

## Why the same tickets cost different amounts on different sellers

Price differences are not only about timing. At any given moment, similar or even identical seats can have very different totals depending on where you look:

- **Fees.** Service and delivery fees vary between marketplaces. Some show them up front, others only at the last step. Compare totals, never the listed price.

- **Currency.** International tours and events sell through sites in several countries and currencies. Exchange rates and card fees change what you actually pay.

- **Different sellers and channels.** Different countries get access to different inventory, channels and promotions, so the same event can have different listings and prices depending on where it is searched from.

- **Listing quality.** Two listings for the same section may differ in row, view or delivery date. Lower is not a saving unless it is the same thing.

International demand makes this more visible. A festival in one country or a European leg of a world tour can draw buyers from many countries at once, each seeing listings through the sites and currencies available to them. Two fans sitting next to each other may have paid quite different totals for the same row, simply because they bought through different channels.

## Practical tips for paying less on event tickets

- **Check primary first.** Extra releases at face value happen more than people expect, especially close to the date.

- **Compare totals, not listed prices.** Fees and currency conversion can reverse the order of two listings.

- **Know the transfer method.** Mobile transfer, name-tied tickets and delivery dates all matter. A ticket you cannot get into the venue with is worth nothing.

- **Buy from marketplaces with buyer guarantees.** Avoid paying strangers directly by bank transfer.

- **Set your own price and stick to it.** Waiting for a last-minute drop is a bet. Decide in advance how much risk you will accept.

- **Read the refund terms.** They decide whether a better price after you buy is any use to you.

## Buying tickets for a group or a team

Groups face a narrower market. The more seats you need together, the fewer listings match, and the less room there is to wait for a drop. Decide early whether sitting together matters more than price. Companies sending staff to conferences and trade shows face the same choice for passes and hotels, and booking refundable options early, then checking again, works for both.

TipIf you are buying several seats together, check whether the marketplace lets you split a listing. Two separate pairs a row apart sometimes cost less than four seats in one listing.

Event ticket prices drop when supply outruns demand, and rise when it does not. What you can control is paying the lowest total available for those exact tickets, from anywhere.

## Frequently asked questions

### When do event ticket prices drop?

Resale prices often drop when there are many unsold listings, especially in the final days before an event, or when the promoter releases more tickets. For high-demand events they can stay high or rise.

### What is the cheapest time to buy concert tickets?

It depends on supply and demand for that show. Large venues with many listings often get cheaper close to the date, while small sold-out shows rarely do. Check primary sales for extra releases before buying on resale.

### Why do resale ticket prices differ between sellers?

Each seller sets a price and each marketplace adds its own fees. Currency, card fees, and the different inventory and channels available from different countries also change the total.

### Is it safe to buy resale tickets?

It is safer on marketplaces with buyer guarantees. Check the seat details, transfer method and any rules that tie tickets to the buyer's name, and avoid paying strangers by bank transfer.
