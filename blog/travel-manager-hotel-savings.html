How to reduce corporate hotel spend: a travel manager's guide

Hotels are one of the two largest lines in most corporate travel budgets, next to airfare, and they are harder to control. Prices change daily, the same hotel is sold through many channels, and travelers book on their own schedule. Negotiated programs help, but they leave gaps. This guide is for travel managers who want to reduce corporate hotel spend without moving travelers to worse hotels.

Where corporate hotel spend leaks

Most hotel overspend comes from a handful of patterns. Knowing which ones apply to your program tells you where to start.

Rate leakage

Rate leakage has two forms. The first is out-of-channel booking: travelers book outside the company's booking channel, so the negotiated rate is never applied and the nights do not count toward the volume you committed to a hotel chain. The second is in-channel leakage: the booking goes through the right channel but at a higher rate than the one negotiated, because the negotiated rate was not loaded, was not available on those dates, or the traveler picked a different rate type.

Both forms show up in your data. Match booked rates against negotiated rates for the same hotels and dates, and look for hotel nights in expense reports that have no matching booking record.

Non-refundable rates on trips that change

Non-refundable rates are usually priced below flexible ones. Travelers pick them to look cost-conscious, then the meeting moves. One lost night can wipe out the discount from several bookings.

Booked once, never checked again

Hotel prices keep moving after a booking is made. A traveler who booked a refundable rate three weeks out could often rebook the identical hotel for less a week later, but nobody looks.

One country, one search

Travelers and booking channels search from your own country. The same hotel can be priced differently when searched from another country, and a single-country search never finds that price.

Corporate hotel rates vs OTA rates

A negotiated corporate rate is a commitment from the hotel to a set price or discount, usually agreed in an annual RFP. Public rates on online travel agencies (OTAs) and hotel websites change daily. The practical question is which one is lower on the dates your travelers actually book.

Refundable rates and re-shopping

Re-shopping means checking the price of a booked hotel again before the cancellation deadline and rebooking if it has dropped. It only works with refundable rates, so the two policies belong together.

An illustrative example: a traveler books a refundable rate at $240 a night for four nights, three weeks before arrival. Ten days later the identical hotel, dates and rate type are available for $210. Rebooking saves $120 on one trip. A non-refundable rate at $225 would have looked like the better deal on the day of booking, and would have ended up costing $60 more.

Rules that keep re-shopping safe:

  • Match the hotel, dates, guests, rate type, meal plan and cancellation terms exactly.
  • Compare the final total with all taxes and fees included.
  • Confirm the new booking before cancelling the original.
  • Watch deadlines, which are often set in the hotel's local time.
  • Make sure the new confirmation reaches the expense report and your duty-of-care records.

Geographic arbitrage for corporate travel

Geographic arbitrage means buying the same item from the country where it is priced lowest. For hotels, different countries get access to different inventory, channels, local rates and promotions, so the identical hotel can cost less when searched from another country. The spread is often 5-30%.

In a test of 1,000 hotels searched from 100 countries, 80% of countries showed a lower price than the traveler's own country, with a typical gap of 20-40%. The obstacle is effort. Checking one hotel from 195 countries by hand, one VPN connection at a time, and verifying taxes and fees for each result, is impossible in practice for a single trip, let alone a whole program. A practical playbook for travel managers

  1. Baseline your top destinations.Pull hotel spend by city and hotel for the last 12 months. A small number of cities often accounts for most of the money.
  2. Measure leakage.Compare booked rates with negotiated rates, and expense data with booking data.
  3. Make refundable the default.Use it for trips booked more than a few days out. Allow non-refundable only when plans are firm and the discount is large.
  4. Allow identical-hotel savings in policy.If a traveler finds the same approved hotel for less, the policy should welcome it, with a clear note on how to record the booking.
  5. Check volume commitments.Find out whether nights booked outside your preferred channel still count toward targets agreed with hotel chains, and weigh that against the savings.
  6. Run a pilot.Pick a group of frequent travelers for one quarter and compare what they paid with what they would have paid.

Frequently asked questions

What is rate leakage in corporate travel?

Rate leakage is hotel spend that misses the negotiated program. It happens when travelers book outside the company channel, or when bookings in the right channel end up at a higher rate than the negotiated one.

Are corporate hotel rates always lower than OTA rates?

No. Static negotiated rates protect against peak prices but can sit above public OTA prices on quiet dates, and the same hotel can also be priced lower when searched from another country. Spot audits show where your rates stand.

Should company policy require refundable hotel rates?

For most trips booked more than a few days ahead, yes. Refundable rates protect against changed plans and make re-shopping possible. Non-refundable rates make sense only when plans are firm and the discount is large.

What is re-shopping a hotel booking?

Re-shopping means searching the identical hotel, dates and rate type again before the cancellation deadline. If the price has dropped, the traveler books the lower price, confirms it, and then cancels the original.
